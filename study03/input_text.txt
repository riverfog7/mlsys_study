On Monday morning, a research group met in a small university library to plan a
presentation about language models. They wanted the presentation to begin with
something concrete: a paragraph of ordinary text, a model that people could
recognize, and a measurement that everyone could inspect. Rather than starting
with a collection of performance claims, they decided to follow one document
through the system. Each person brought a different question. One wanted to
understand the vocabulary. Another wanted to know how the model combined
information from different positions. A third was interested in the time spent
waiting for results when the document became longer.

The librarian suggested using a report from a community project. The report
described the renovation of an old railway station into a public meeting place.
It included descriptions of the building, interviews with residents, construction
schedules, and a discussion of how the surrounding streets had changed. A short
excerpt introduced the project, while later paragraphs explained the decisions
in greater detail. This made the document useful for comparing short and long
inputs. The group could take increasingly long prefixes of the same document
without changing the beginning or adding unrelated material between experiments.

Before discussing the model, the group examined the text itself. Words were not
always represented by single tokens. Some common words formed one token, while
longer or less common expressions were divided into several pieces. Spaces and
punctuation also affected the token sequence. The participants wrote down the
number of tokens and kept the token identifiers fixed for each experiment. They
agreed that comparisons would be easier to interpret if the same inputs were
used every time. They also kept a readable preview of the document so that the
numbers did not become detached from the text that produced them.

The first model run was treated as preparation. Loading the weights, creating a
CUDA context, and selecting library routines could all take time before the
machine reached its usual behavior. The group therefore ran the model several
times before recording measurements. During the measured runs, they left the
model in evaluation mode and did not ask it to compute gradients. They used one
document at a time. After each group of repeated runs, they compared the results
and checked that the output remained consistent. A single unusually fast or
slow observation would not be presented as the typical result.

At lunch, the discussion returned to the station renovation. One resident had
argued for retaining the original waiting room, while another had proposed
opening the central wall to create a larger public space. The final design kept
the historical entrance and placed a new reading area beside it. This example
helped the students talk about context. A sentence near the end of a document
could depend on a name, a proposal, or a decision introduced much earlier. The
model needed a way to combine information from different positions, and the
participants wanted to see the actual operations involved in that process.

In the afternoon, the group looked at an execution trace. It contained many small
operations as well as several large matrix multiplications. Some operations
prepared values, some changed their shape, and others combined them. The trace
was not a drawing of an abstract architecture. It was a record of work that had
actually been submitted to the graphics processor. The students followed the
first block and identified the operations associated with attention. They kept
the surrounding projections visible because removing those operations from the
picture would make the measured block different from the one in the model.

One participant asked whether a large allocation automatically meant that an
operation was slow. The group decided to keep those questions separate. Storage
size described how much space a tensor occupied, while execution time described
how long a particular operation took. Data could sometimes be served from a
cache rather than from external memory. A hardware counter could provide another
view by recording traffic at a particular part of the memory system. The group
added these measurements to the trace, taking care to label which numbers came
from the ordinary run and which came from the more detailed profiling session.

They also discussed the limits of the experiment. A small model on one graphics
card did not represent every language model deployment. Processing an entire
document at once was different from generating a single new token while reusing
previous state. Different numeric formats could change both the selected kernels
and the amount of data moved. The students recorded their settings alongside the
figures so that another reader could repeat the experiment or change one setting
at a time. They wanted the figures to support a specific observation rather than
to stand in for a universal claim about all models and all hardware.

By the end of the day, the presentation had a clear starting point. It would show
the input document, the time required by the complete forward pass, and a closer
view of one attention block. The group would explain unfamiliar operations only
when they appeared in the measured execution. If an operation occupied a large
part of the trace, they would investigate its data access and arithmetic before
suggesting a change. If an expected bottleneck did not appear, they would report
that result as well. The purpose of the exercise was to understand the machine
that was actually running in front of them and to build explanations from its
observed behavior.
